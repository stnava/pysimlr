3.1 Study Design, Biomedical Cohorts, and Synthetic Ground-Truth Regimes

We evaluate the seven benchmarked architectures defined in Chapter 2 across 12 multi-view benchmarks: six real biomedical and clinical cohorts (ADNI-MCI, MultiOmics [TCGA-BRCA], TCGA-KIRC, Heart, Diabetes, and mfeat) and six synthetic generative regimes with known ground-truth latent coordinates \(\mathbf{U}^*\) and feature supports \(\mathbf{V}_m^*\) (Table 3.1). Every model is evaluated across 3 independent 70/30 stratified or random train/test splits (seeds \(42, 43, 44\)) across three nsa_flow quantile sparsity levels (\(q \in \{\text{None}, 0.5, 0.8\}\), 756 train/test fits), plus 189 paired reruns with non-negative loadings on the three high-dimensional cohorts and a 1,025-configuration hyperparameter sweep on ADNI-MCI (\(N = 1,084\), one seed). To prevent data leakage, all feature standardization (StandardScaler), column completeness filtering (\(\ge 98\%\)), and demographic covariate adjustments (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell residualization (Frisch and Waugh 1933; Lovell 1963) of age, biological sex, and education inside ADNI-MCI) are fitted strictly within each training split and applied out-of-sample to the held-out test split.

In ADNI-MCI (\(N = 1,084\)) (Mueller et al. 2005; Jack et al. 2008; Weiner et al. 2017), Views 0–2 capture \(382\) regional structural MRI features from the ANTsPyT1w/ANTsPyMM pipelines: the volumes of 160 hierarchical segmentation labels (View 0: cortical, subcortical, ventricular, white-matter and cerebellar labels), the volume-to-surface-area ratio of the same 160 labels (View 1; for a cortical label it tracks thickness, otherwise it is a shape index), and DiReCT cortical thickness averaged in each of 62 Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) cortical regions (View 2) (Avants et al. 2008, 2011; Tustison et al. 2014; Desikan et al. 2006; Klein and Tourville 2012; Stone et al. 2020, 2024; Marek et al. 2011; Smith et al. 2013), while View 3 comprises eleven neuropsychological scales (ADAS11, ADAS13, ADASQ4 (Rosen et al. 1984), MMSE (Folstein et al. 1975), CDRSB (Morris 1993), TRABSCOR (Reitan 1958), FAQ (Pfeffer et al. 1982), and four Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test sub-scores RAVLT_immediate, RAVLT_learning, RAVLT_forgetting, RAVLT_perc_forgetting (Rey 1964)). Following Protocol B, the three MRI views align with the cognitive battery during training, but test-set predictions are generated exclusively from structural MRI. In MultiOmics (TCGA-BRCA, \(N =\) 822) (The Cancer Genome Atlas Network 2012) and TCGA-KIRC (\(N = 467\)) (The Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2013), mRNA expression (\(p_1 = 200\)), somatic copy-number variation (\(p_2 = 150\)), and RPPA proteomics (\(p_3 = 150\)) predict clinical Estrogen Receptor status and pathologic tumor stage, respectively.

3.2 Global 12-Dataset Benchmark: Subspace Capacity vs. Single-Axis Interpretability

Across the \(B =\) 12 dataset blocks and seven architectures, block-averaged non-parametric Friedman (Friedman 1937) and Iman–Davenport (Iman and Davenport 1980) omnibus tests differentiate the seven models on both the linear subspace probe (\(\chi_F^2 =\) 14.85, \(p =\) \(2.15 \times 10^{-2}\); \(F_F =\) 2.86, \(p =\) \(1.55 \times 10^{-2}\)) and the multi-modal consensus probe (\(\chi_F^2 =\) 13.43, \(p =\) \(3.67 \times 10^{-2}\); \(F_F =\) 2.52, \(p =\) \(2.94 \times 10^{-2}\); Nemenyi Critical Difference \(CD =\) 2.600 at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (Demšar 2006; Nemenyi 1963; Benavoli et al. 2016)). Table 3.2 summarizes the global metrics across all seven models, accompanied by the Demšar Critical Difference diagram (Figure 3.1), the multi-objective speed–accuracy and support–consensus Pareto frontiers (Figure 3.2), and the three-probe performance profile (Figure 3.4).

Three empirical principles emerge from Table 3.2 and Figures 3.1–3.4:

  1. Multi-View Alignment Against Single-Matrix Factorization: The leading mean Friedman ranks on the linear entrypoint probe are NED (3.00), NEDPP (3.33), Flow-SiMR-V (3.58), LEND (3.58), against PCA (5.38) and NSAFlow-PCA (5.42) (Figure 3.3). On heterogeneous cohorts such as mfeat (\(p = [76, 64, 6, 47]\)) and Shared+Private (\(p = [160, 60, 30]\)), concatenated PCA reaches a linear score of 0.727 (mfeat accuracy) and 0.291 (Shared+Private \(R^2\)), against 0.973 (NED) and 0.949 (SiMLR) for the best multi-view model. Over the 12 datasets (seeds averaged, Wilcoxon signed-rank against PCA with Holm adjustment, Appendix B), the linear score differs from PCA at \(p < 0.05\) for 0 of 6 non-PCA models.
  2. Coordinate Alignment and the Random Forest Axis Drop: As established in Equation 1.2, ridge regression on \(\mathbf{X}_m \mathbf{V}_m\) is insensitive to orthogonal or oblique column mixing \(\mathbf{V}_m \mathbf{M}\), hiding the fact that dense PCA mixes distinct mechanisms across columns. With coordinate-splitting decision trees (RandomForest), PCA loses -0.044 on average across the 12 cohorts (Random Forest minus linear score), falling from 0.125 to -0.000 on the weak sparse factors of NonnegPartsWeak and from 0.148 to 0.042 on ADNI-MCI (72% lower). The smallest drops are SiMLR (-0.017), NEDPP (-0.020).
  3. Operating Points and Feature Density (\(q = 0.5\) vs. \(q = 0.8\)): Figure 3.5 shows the quantile threshold \(q\) as a lens on feature support recovery without conflating scales on secondary axes. Across the 756 default-positivity fits, the mean linear score is 0.713, 0.716 and 0.715 at \(q = \text{None}\), \(0.5\) and \(0.8\), and the mean Random Forest axis score is 0.687, 0.689 and 0.688; at \(q = 0.8\), 57.8% of loadings are zero while 99.8% of the best linear score is retained. At \(q = 0.5\), support recovery on the \(50\%\)-sparse Linear regime ranges from \(F_1 =\) 0.500 (PCA) to 1.000 (NSAFlow-PCA), and reaches 0.875 on NonnegParts (Flow-SiMR-V). On the focal \(20\%\)-active modules, the best \(F_1\) at \(q = 0.8\) is 0.831 on NonnegPartsWeak (Flow-SiMR-V) and 0.660 on Shared+Private (Flow-SiMR-V).

3.3 Non-Subtractive Sparsification and Two-Stage Uniform Shrinkage: Synthetic Verification (Propositions 1–3)

Numerical checks and 20-seed synthetic benchmarks verify Propositions 1–3 and two-stage support-restricted uniform shrinkage (results_cache/v043_synthetic_theorems.json from scripts/benchmark_synthetic_theorems.py; Section 2.3.1; Section 2.4; Donoho and Johnstone (1994)).

  1. Proposition 1 (Subtractive Shrinkage Cancellation, Equation 2.5): Across shrinkage levels \(\alpha \in [0, 0.99]\) on \(p = 100\) coordinates at \(q = 0.30\), order-statistic quantile soft-thresholding (sparseness_alg="soft") yields maximum \(\ell_\infty\) deviation \(1.07 \times 10^{-15}\) from the \(\alpha = 0\) support and weights, confirming exact \(\alpha\)-cancellation. Under uniform-shrunk coordinate floors, continuous pre-retraction soft-thresholding (sparseness_type="soft", _apply_soft_threshold_sparseness) suffers floor erosion: at \(\alpha = 0.99\), the uniform floor reaches 0.0826 above the threshold \(\tau =\) 0.0415, pulling cosine similarity with \(\alpha = 0\) down to 0.666 (Figure 3.6 A).
  2. Proposition 2 (Exact Top-\(m\) Cardinality and Radial Simplex Normalization, Equation 2.6): On \(p = 60, m = 20\), exact cardinality-constrained projection preserves the Cauchy–Schwarz variance bound with absolute error \(0.00 \times 10^{0}\) and achieves lower Euclidean squared error (68.53 vs. 69.24) and higher cosine similarity (0.894 vs. 0.855) than shifted soft-thresholding. At sparseness=None (\(p = 100\)), legacy Euclidean simplex projection subtracts \(\theta =\) 0.1285, zeros 81% of active coordinates, and lowers cosine similarity with the input direction to 0.718, whereas radial simplex normalization retains cosine 1.000 with zero dropped coordinates (Figure 3.6 B).
  3. Proposition 3 (Column-Support Uniform Shrinkage Orthogonality, Equation 2.7): On \(k = 2\) disjoint column supports of size [40, 40], ColumnSupportShrinkageOperator maintains exact orthogonality across \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) with maximum Gram Frobenius error \(9.99 \times 10^{-16}\), whereas global UniformShrinkageOperator mixes the supports until Frobenius error reaches 1.414 and column cosine reaches 1.000 at \(\alpha = 1\) (Figure 3.6 C).

We next evaluate two-stage support-restricted uniform shrinkage (fit_uniform_shrunk, Donoho–Johnstone Risk Inflation Criterion ric Equation 2.8 vs. plugin shrinkage) across 20 seeds in a four-view generative setting (\(M = 4\), view dimensions (80, 100, 60, 160), \(N_{\text{train}} =\) 60, \(N_{\text{test}} =\) 200, block-correlated noise \(\rho = 0.45\) in blocks of size \(5\)). Design A tests rank \(k = 1\) with 30% active positive signal per view (Table 3.3); Design B tests rank \(k = 2\) with disjoint 25%/25% active supports per view (Table 3.4; Figure 3.7).

Across the 20 seeds in Design A (\(k = 1\), two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests), two-stage conical shrinkage at \(q = 0.5\) with plugin \(\alpha\) achieves \(R^2 =\) 0.898 \(\pm\) 0.011 and \(F_1 =\) 0.750 \(\pm\) 0.001 against global uniform shrinkage (0.829 \(\pm\) 0.019, \(F_1 =\) 0.462 \(\pm\) 0.000; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.069, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.288, \(p =\) \(1.19 \times 10^{-5}\)) and exceeds legacy soft-thresholding (0.880 \(\pm\) 0.014; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.018, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\)). Under theoretical RIC shrinkage at \(q = 0.5\), two-stage conical attains \(R^2 =\) 0.878 \(\pm\) 0.014 and \(F_1 =\) 0.750 \(\pm\) 0.001 (\(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.048, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.288, \(p =\) \(1.19 \times 10^{-5}\)). Two-stage radial convex (\(q = 0.5\), plugin) reaches \(R^2 =\) 0.898 \(\pm\) 0.011 and \(F_1 =\) 0.749 \(\pm\) 0.002 against legacy Euclidean convex (0.891 \(\pm\) 0.012, \(F_1 =\) 0.744 \(\pm\) 0.033; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.008, \(p =\) \(4.77 \times 10^{-5}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.005, \(p =\) \(2.16 \times 10^{-1}\)).

In Design B (\(k = 2\) disjoint supports), two-stage conical shrinkage (\(q = 0.5\), RIC) achieves mean \(R^2 =\) 0.872 \(\pm\) 0.098 and \(F_1 =\) 0.811 \(\pm\) 0.107 with exact orthogonality (fro = 0.0000, cos = 0.0000), outperforming global uniform shrinkage (0.845 \(\pm\) 0.080, \(F_1 =\) 0.520 \(\pm\) 0.033, fro = 0.1617; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.027, \(p =\) \(7.30 \times 10^{-3}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.290, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\); \(\Delta \text{Fro} =\) -0.162, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\)) and legacy conical hard thresholding (0.842 \(\pm\) 0.070, \(F_1 =\) 0.677 \(\pm\) 0.017, fro = 0.1333; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.030, \(p =\) \(1.21 \times 10^{-3}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.133, \(p =\) \(8.51 \times 10^{-4}\)). Two-stage radial convex (\(q = 0.5\), RIC) attains \(R^2 =\) 0.885 \(\pm\) 0.036 and \(F_1 =\) 0.817 \(\pm\) 0.075 (fro = 0.0000), improving over legacy Euclidean convex (0.855 \(\pm\) 0.063, \(F_1 =\) 0.673 \(\pm\) 0.046, fro = 0.0853; \(\Delta R^2 =\) +0.030, \(p =\) \(8.20 \times 10^{-5}\); \(\Delta F_1 =\) +0.144, \(p =\) \(1.91 \times 10^{-6}\)).

3.4 Clinical Neuroimaging in ADNI-MCI (\(N = 1,084\)): Predicting Cognition from MRI Alone and Attributing the Prediction

A primary clinical test of multi-modal representation learning is whether aligning structural neuroimaging with multi-domain cognitive batteries during training yields an anatomical basis that better predicts cognitive severity in new patients who have only undergone an MRI scan (Protocol B). Across \(N = 1,084\) participants with Mild Cognitive Impairment, Table 3.5 compares all seven benchmark models on held-out MRI-only prediction of composite cognitive impairment (CogPC1 \(R^2\), after in-fold adjustment for age, sex, and education), single-axis Random Forest prediction, and mean ridge regression \(R^2\) across all eleven individual neuropsychological scales (ADAS11, ADAS13, ADASQ4, MMSE, CDRSB, TRABSCOR, FAQ, and four RAVLT memory scales).

Comparing the unsupervised anatomical baselines against cognitive-aligned SiMR models in Table 3.5 and Figures 3.8–3.13 yields four findings:

  1. Supervising MRI Factor Discovery with Cognitive Batteries Improves Out-of-Sample MRI Prediction: Unsupervised PCA on the \(382\) structural MRI features explains \(R^2 =\) 0.148 \(\pm\) 0.010 of out-of-sample cognitive variance (CogPC1) on the linear probe and 0.042 on the single-axis Random Forest probe. Aligning the MRI projections with the 11-scale cognitive battery during training gives, for the four leading models (3-seed means \(\pm\) SD), NED (0.209 \(\pm\) 0.027), Flow-SiMR-V (0.198 \(\pm\) 0.021), NEDPP (0.192 \(\pm\) 0.022), LEND (0.192 \(\pm\) 0.029); the best is +41.2% over PCA. The best single-axis Random Forest score is 0.155 (Flow-SiMR-V), 3.7 times that of PCA. In the single-seed sweep the best configuration reaches 0.2686 (Flow-SiMR-V) and the best linear-family configuration 0.2595 (SiMLR), against 0.1869 for PCA; mean prediction across all eleven scales reaches 0.124 (Flow-SiMR-V) against 0.085 for PCA (+46.0%). These sweep peaks are maxima over many configurations on one split and are optimistic.
  2. Which MRI Features Carry the Prediction: Figure 3.11 attributes the held-out CogPC1 prediction of the non-negative \(q = 0.85\) fit (all nine MRI columns, \(R^2 =\) 0.170). One column per MRI view carries it. Alone, the predictive column gives \(R^2 =\) 0.122 in volume (component 1), 0.195 in vol/area (component 1) and 0.084 in DiReCT thickness (component 2, 1 region), against at most 0.068 for any other column, and only the vol/area column is not redundant with the rest (unique \(R^2\) +0.040). Component numbers are not identifiable across fits, so the refits below follow each view’s predictive column rather than a fixed index. Across 20 half-sample refits, the predictive vol/area basis selects these features in at least half of them (fraction of refits): L entorhinal (DKT parcellation): 0.95; L temporal lobe: 0.90; L inferior parietal (DKT cortex label): 0.85; L entorhinal (DKT cortex label): 0.80; R entorhinal (DKT cortex label): 0.80; R entorhinal (DKT parcellation): 0.75. These are mostly entorhinal, temporal-lobe and inferior parietal measures, the regions where atrophy is expected in prodromal Alzheimer’s disease (Dickerson et al. 2009; Jack et al. 2018). DiReCT thickness tells the same story less clearly. Its first principal component explains 58% of the variance across the 62 regions (mean inter-region correlation 0.55), and consensus component 1 correlates 0.87 with subject-mean thickness, so hard-assigning each region to its largest structure coefficient places 62 / 0 / 0 regions on components 1 / 2 / 3: a display of the global factor, not of localisation (Panel B). The thickness basis itself is unstable under the default collapse penalty: it keeps two or fewer regions in 8 of 20 refits (median held-out \(R^2\) 0.118). Non-negative region sums on positively correlated features typically correlate above the penalty’s threshold of 0.3, so the penalty pushes those columns towards one or two regions. With collapse_weight = 0 the predictive thickness basis keeps a median of 9 regions (two or fewer in 1 refit), its median \(R^2\) rises to 0.144, and its most frequent regions are (fraction of refits) L entorhinal: 0.55; L supramarginal: 0.45 (Panel D). No single thickness region replicates in more than 0.55 of refits, so the thickness map is a pattern, not a list of identified regions (docs/audit/evidence/adni_global_factor_check.csv). For reference, unthresholded non-negative fits are 67% sparse on the DKT view without rotation and 67% with Varimax, against 0% for dense PCA.
  3. Support-Shuffled and PCA-Rotated Score Controls: To separate coordinate-level axis alignment and anatomical support localization from subspace capacity, two negative controls are evaluated across 3 seeds on ADNI-MCI (adni_mci_axis_alignment_controls.csv, axis_alignment_control_metrics): (i) a PCA-rotated score control rotates the learned scores \(\mathbf{Z} = [\mathbf{X}_m \mathbf{V}_m]\) by their in-fold principal-component eigenvectors, which leaves the linear probe unchanged (largest difference \(1.08 \times 10^{-7}\)) and lowers the single-axis Random Forest score for 2 of 5 models; (ii) a support-shuffled basis control permutes the rows of each \(\mathbf{V}_m\) within its view, preserving sparsity, weight magnitudes and \(\mathbf{V}_m^\top \mathbf{V}_m\), and lowers the Random Forest CogPC1 \(R^2\) for 5 of 5 models: Flow-SiMR-V (0.151 \(\to\) 0.091), LEND (0.119 \(\to\) 0.059), NEDPP (0.134 \(\to\) 0.096), NSAFlow-PCA (0.033 \(\to\) -0.059), SiMLR (0.094 \(\to\) 0.028). Unshuffled Random Forest scores are 1.0 to 4.8 times that of PCA.
  4. Rank Selection, Component Identifiability, and Display Policy: Evaluating latent rank selection via select_rank(..., criterion="latent_reliability") (Section 2.7; Equation 2.10) across 20 half-sample refits (docs/audit/evidence/adni_rank_selection.csv, selection margin 0.2) selects \(k =\) 2 under non-negative estimation (positivity="positive") and \(k =\) 2 under signed estimation (positivity="either"). At \(k = 2\) under non-negativity, out-of-bag univariate latent reliabilities are 0.891 and 0.975 (gains 0.438 and 0.630), multivariate reliability is 0.945 (gain 0.520), and all_coupled = True (passed = True). At \(k = 3\) under non-negativity, univariate reliabilities are 0.928, 0.502, and 0.920 (gains 0.516, 0.219, and 0.652): Component 2 drops to minimum reliability 0.502 (gain 0.219), multivariate reliability drops to 0.790 (gain declines from 0.520 at \(k = 2\) to 0.402, failing multivariate monotonicity), and all_coupled = False (passed = False), so the fixed \(k = 3\) diagnostic fits below isolate Components 1 and 3 as the two high-reliability axes. Under signed estimation (positivity="either"), \(k = 2\) achieves reliabilities 0.697 and 0.603 (multivariate gain 0.456, passed = True), whereas at \(k = 3\) reliabilities drop to 0.882, 0.645, and 0.197 (minimum gain -0.053, multivariate gain 0.362, passed = False).
    • Signed Displays Protocol: Across all displays in this manuscript: (i) feature maps display only features whose 95% bootstrap confidence interval (from whole-pipeline refits in loading_inference) strictly excludes zero; (ii) named biomarker panels report features selected via Meinshausen–Bühlmann stability selection at threshold \(\pi_{\text{thr}} = 0.80\) with expected false selections \(E[\text{false}] \le 1.0\); and (iii) non-negative displays present hard-assignment OLEE loadings accompanied by the loading_margin certainty metric.

3.5 Multi-Omic Oncology (TCGA-BRCA, TCGA-KIRC) and Patient-Level Clinical Attribution

To verify that the same architectural principles generalize beyond neuroimaging to molecular oncology and clinical tabular medicine, Table 3.6 compares all seven models across the six real cohorts, Figure 3.14 reports cross-cohort linear entrypoint scores (pysimlr.plot_benchmark), Figure 3.15 and Figure 3.16 map reproducible feature-to-component structure (pysimlr.plot_top_features and pysimlr.plot_feature_chord), and Figure 3.17 displays the hard-assigned OLEE loadings \(\mathbf{L}_m\) across Heart and Diabetes modalities.

In MultiOmics (TCGA-BRCA, \(N =\) 822), out-of-sample Estrogen Receptor classification accuracy on the linear entrypoint is 92.5% \(\pm\) 1.2% (Flow-SiMR-V), 89.9% \(\pm\) 1.2% (LEND) and 92.2% \(\pm\) 1.3% (NEDPP), against 91.5% \(\pm\) 1.8% for PCA (3-seed means \(\pm\) SD); the sparse non-negative dictionaries \(\mathbf{V}_m\), tri-modal feature chord, and view-level quality radar are examined in Appendix A. In TCGA-KIRC (\(N = 467\)), pathologic stage classification on the consensus embedding \(\mathbf{U}\) is 64.1% \(\pm\) 1.1% for LEND against 67.1% \(\pm\) 1.5% for PCA. On compact clinical cohorts, Diabetes (\(N =\) 442) reaches \(R^2 =\) 0.352 for SiMLR against 0.351 for PCA, and Heart (\(N =\) 297) reaches accuracy 0.848 for NSAFlow-PCA and 0.819 for SiMLR. Figure 3.15, Figure 3.16, and Figure 3.17 show the stability-selected structure correlations, multi-view feature-to-component chord, and OLEE loadings \(\mathbf{L}_m\) across Heart (out-of-bag latent_reliability 0.73 and 0.57) and Diabetes (latent_reliability 0.95 and 0.83); while the linear entrypoint \(\mathbf{Z}_{m,\text{lin}} = \mathbf{X}_m \mathbf{V}_m\) maps individual observations into the latent consensus space, the OLEE loadings \(\mathbf{L}_m\) decompose individual patient profiles into exact additive risk attributions \(c_{m,i,j,l} = x_{m,i,j} l_{m,j,l}\) without post-hoc Monte Carlo approximations (Wachter et al. 2018; Karimi et al. 2021; Pearl 2009). Complete parameter sensitivity ablations (the \(w \in [0.01, 0.95]\) trade-off curve, basis diagnostics, stability rank selection across \(k\), and synthetic noise recovery) are in Appendix B.

3.6 Re-Benchmarking on Six Biomedical Cohorts (v0.43.1): Non-Subtractive Sparsification, Radial Simplex, and Uniform Shrinkage

To measure the empirical impact of Propositions 1–3 on real biomedical cohorts (pysimlr v0.43.1, scripts/run_real_data_v043.py), we re-benchmark standard simlr (Part A) and evaluate two-stage uniform shrinkage fit_uniform_shrunk (Part B) across all six real cohorts (ADNI-MCI, MultiOmics [TCGA-BRCA], TCGA-KIRC, Heart, Diabetes, mfeat) over three random seeds (42, 43, 44).

In Part A (part_a_comparison_summary.csv, Table 3.7), 107 of 108 simlr fits (99.1%) converge; the single non-convergent run is TCGA-KIRC under positivity="either", sparseness=None, seed 44 (Stop_Reason = "max_iter"). Across the 81 matched fits (27 cohort-by-positivity-by-sparseness cells \(\times\) 3 seeds) comparing v0.43.1 against the pre-v0.43 cache, signed positivity="either" improves mean consensus metric by +0.007 (+0.003 at \(q = 0.5\) and +0.019 at \(q = 0.8\)), while non-negative positivity="positive" ranges from -0.026 to +0.007 (\(\max |\Delta| =\) 0.026). Under radial simplex normalization (positivity="convex") on the three high-dimensional biomedical cohorts, MultiOmics (TCGA-BRCA) reaches peak consensus accuracy 0.894 and mean linear accuracy 0.916 (mean consensus 0.890), ahead of positivity="positive" (peak consensus 0.870, mean linear 0.895) and positivity="either" (peak consensus 0.836, mean linear 0.824).

In Part B (part_b_uniform_shrunk_summary.csv, Table 3.8; Figure 3.18), we evaluate 96 fit_uniform_shrunk runs across all six cohorts and ranks \(k \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) (capped at \(k \in \{1, 2\}\) for Heart and Diabetes). Under positivity="conical", 48 of 48 fits converge with maximum Gram defect \(D(\mathbf{V}_m) \le\) \(3.79 \times 10^{-14}\) and maximum inter-column cosine 0.000. Under radial simplex positivity="convex", 45 of 48 fits converge with maximum inter-column cosine 0.000 and maximum Gram defect 0.482 (non-zero defect arises because radial \(\ell_1\)-simplex columns have unequal \(\ell_2\) norms when disjoint support sizes differ). The three non-converged convex fits are all on Diabetes (\(p = [5, 5]\)) at \(k = 2\) (Stop_Reason = "no_descent" across seeds 42, 43, 44, consensus 0.273, linear 0.353), whereas positivity="conical" converges in all \(3/3\) splits with consensus 0.331, linear 0.405, and Gram defect \(1.78 \times 10^{-15}\). Pooled across cohorts for conical, mean consensus score rises from 0.463 (\(k = 1\), linear 0.524) to 0.530 (\(k = 2\), linear 0.641) and 0.572 (\(k = 3\), linear 0.681). At each cohort’s target rank (\(k = 2\) for Heart and Diabetes, \(k = 3\) for ADNI-MCI, MultiOmics, TCGA-KIRC, and mfeat), fit_uniform_shrunk (conical) reaches linear scores of 0.925 (MultiOmics), 0.931 (mfeat), 0.830 (Heart), 0.662 (TCGA-KIRC), 0.405 (Diabetes), and 0.209 (ADNI-MCI).
